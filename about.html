What are Diastereomers?

Many amino acids, such as phenylalanine, exist in two stereochemical configurations, L and D. These form enantiomers: non-superimposable mirror images, analogous to left and right hands. Peptides containing multiple stereogenic centers can also form diastereomers, stereoisomers that are not mirror images of one another. Replacing one L-amino acid with its D-counterpart produces a peptide with identical amino acid identities and connectivity but a distinct three-dimensional configuration. Motivation

Do molecular machine learning models actually learn stereochemistry? Deep learning has reshaped molecular and biological modeling: structure predictors reach near-experimental accuracy, protein language models learn evolutionary and structural priors from sequence alone, and generative methods design proteins de novo. Yet models trained predominantly on naturally occurring sequences may exploit sequence and composition statistics without adequately capturing the effects of stereochemical variation. L- and D-amino acids share identical atomic composition and connectivity, so representations based solely on sequence, residue identity, or composition are blind to stereochemical changes, even when those changes measurably alter physicochemical properties.

Three factors have left this gap largely unaddressed. Public peptide datasets are derived almost entirely from natural proteomes and therefore contain little stereochemical variation. Established retention-time predictors were built for conventional proteomics and do not explicitly distinguish L- and D-configurations. And building large, stereochemically controlled peptide libraries has historically been experimentally impractical.

Recent advances in high-throughput flow synthesis now make it possible to produce tens of thousands of synthetic peptides in a standardized way. StereoPep uses this capability to create 48,788 experimentally characterized peptides with matched diastereomeric pairs, enabling a direct test of whether specialized retention-time predictors, molecular fingerprints, graph neural networks, sequence transformers, and protein and chemical language models are sensitive to stereochemical inversions. Beyond benchmarking, the retention-time shifts induced by stereochemical tags point toward a new multiplexing strategy for LC-MS proteomics.

Our central finding is a dissociation between overall retention-time prediction and stereochemical discrimination: the strongest predictors reach Pearson r ≈ 0.80 overall yet perform near chance on diastereomeric pair discrimination. Explicitly encoding chirality at the input level, through dedicated D-Phe tokens, atom-level chirality features, or SMILES stereodescriptors, is not sufficient to close this gap. We hope StereoPep drives the development of chirality-aware architectures, 3D and conformer-aware representations, and stereochemistry-aware pretraining.
